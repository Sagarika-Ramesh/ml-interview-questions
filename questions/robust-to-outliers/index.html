Question 1 RobustnessVery common

How do you make a model more robust to outliers?

The 30-second answer

First I'd find out whether the outliers are errors or real rare events. I'd fix or drop errors and keep real extremes. Then I'd limit how much any single point can pull the model: transform or clip skewed features, scale with the median and interquartile range (IQR), and swap mean squared error (MSE) for Huber or mean absolute error (MAE) loss. Tree-based models help with odd feature values. Finally I'd evaluate with MAE as well as root mean squared error (RMSE) and look at the worst residuals.

Start here

First question: error, or a real rare event?

"Outlier" covers two very different things, and the right fix depends on which one you have. A sensor reading of −999 is an error. A $40,000 purchase in a fraud dataset is probably the most important row you have.

👉

Sometimes the outliers are the task. In fraud or anomaly detection you don't make the model ignore them. You model them directly, for example with Isolation Forest or class weighting.

Layer 1 · the data

Find them, then decide what to do

For a single feature, simple rules work. When a point is only odd as a combination of features (a 1.9 m tall person weighing 40 kg), you need a multivariate method.

MethodLooks atGood to know
Interquartile range (IQR) fences1 featureFlags points beyond Q1 − 1.5·IQR or Q3 + 1.5·IQR. No normality assumption.
z-score1 feature|z| > 3 is common, but the mean and standard deviation are pulled by the very outliers you're hunting. The modified z-score uses the median and median absolute deviation (MAD) instead.
Mahalanobis distancemanyDistance that accounts for correlation. Assumes a roughly elliptical shape.
Isolation ForestmanyRandom splits isolate outliers in fewer steps. Fast and works on high-dimensional tabular data.
Local Outlier Factor (LOF)manyCompares local density with the neighbours'. Catches points that are odd only within their own cluster.

Then handle them

  • Remove only when you're confident it's an error, and record how many rows you dropped.
  • Clip (winsorize) to, say, the 1st and 99th percentiles. You keep the row but cap its pull.
  • Transform skewed features with log, Box-Cox or Yeo-Johnson. After the transform, a heavy tail stops looking extreme.
  • Scale robustly with RobustScaler (median and interquartile range) instead of StandardScaler (mean and std).
  • Rank or bin with QuantileTransformer, so only the order of values matters.
⚠️

Compute percentiles and scalers on the training set only. Fitting them on all the data leaks test-set information into training.

Layer 2 · the loss function

Stop squaring big errors

This is often the single biggest improvement. Mean squared error (MSE) squares each error, so one point that's 10 units off counts as much as 100 points that are 1 unit off. The model bends toward that one point.

Robust losses grow more slowly for large errors, so a far-off point still counts, just not quadratically.

LossRobust?Trade-off
Mean squared error (MSE), L2noPredicts the mean. Smooth and fast, but outliers dominate it.
Mean absolute error (MAE), L1yesPredicts the median. Its gradient has the same size for every error and isn't defined at 0.
HuberyesThe usual default. You choose δ, the point where quadratic switches to linear.
Log-coshyesA smooth Huber with no δ to tune, which suits second-order optimisers.
QuantileyesPredicts a chosen quantile, which is handy for prediction intervals.
Tukey / CauchyveryLarge errors barely count at all. Non-convex, so start from a good initial fit.

Classification: outliers are usually wrong labels

In classification, the outliers that hurt are usually mislabeled examples. Cross-entropy pushes hard to fit them. Options: label smoothing, generalized or symmetric cross-entropy, and small-loss selection methods such as co-teaching. Focal loss up-weights hard examples, so it can make label noise worse.

Layer 3 · the model

Some models barely notice outliers

An important distinction: an outlier in the features (X) isn't the same as an outlier in the target (y). Trees are robust to the first kind, but not automatically to the second.

ModelOutliers in XOutliers in yWhy
Decision trees, random forests (RF), gradient boosting machines (GBM)robustdependsSplits use only the order of X. Leaf values are means of y unless you pick a robust objective.

Huber, random sample consensus (RANSAC), Theil-SenvariesrobustRANSAC fits to inliers only. Theil-Sen takes the median of pairwise slopes.
k-nearest neighbours (k-NN), support vector machines (SVM), k-meanssensitivevariesDistance-based, and one huge feature dominates the distance. Scale robustly, or use k-medoids.
Neural netssensitivesensitiveUnbounded activations plus MSE. Normalise the inputs and use a robust loss.
Bayesian models with a Student-t likelihoodvariesrobustHeavy-tailed likelihood: a far-off point is expected occasionally, so it doesn't drag the fit.
Layer 4 · the training loop

Don't let one batch steer the model

  • Regularisation (L1/L2, dropout) stops the model bending to fit individual points.
  • Gradient clipping caps the update size when an outlier-heavy batch produces a huge gradient.
  • Sample reweighting down-weights high-loss points. Iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) does this for linear models.
  • Bagging and ensembles average out the influence of any single point.
  • Early stopping: networks fit clean patterns first and memorise noisy points late in training, so stopping early leaves the noise out.
Layer 5 · how you measure it

Root mean squared error (RMSE) alone can mislead you

RMSE is itself sensitive to outliers. A model can look worse on RMSE because of three rows while being better for 99% of users, or the other way round.

  • Report MAE and median absolute error (MedAE) next to RMSE.
  • Sort by residual and look at the worst 20 rows. They often reveal a data bug.
  • Check whether the outliers all ended up in train or all in test. Stratify or split by time if needed.
  • If the extremes matter to the business, evaluate them as a separate slice.
Be ready for these

Follow-ups interviewers ask

1.Why not just delete the outliers?

Because they may be real, and then the model will be overconfident in production when the same kind of case shows up. Deleting them also shifts the data distribution, can quietly remove an important subgroup, and the deletion rule itself becomes a tuned hyperparameter. Delete errors and keep real events.

2.How do you choose δ for Huber loss?

δ sets where an error stops counting as normal. A common rule is about 1.35 × the residual standard deviation, preferably estimated robustly with the MAD. That keeps about 95% of OLS efficiency when the data really is Gaussian. Otherwise, tune it by cross-validation on a robust metric.

3.Are tree models completely immune to outliers?

No. They're robust to extreme feature values because splits only use the order. With squared loss, a leaf's prediction is still the mean of its targets, so an extreme y skews that leaf, and in boosting it creates large residuals that later trees chase. Use a Huber, MAE or quantile objective.

4.MAE is robust, so why isn't it always used?

Its gradient has the same size whatever the error, so it doesn't slow down near the optimum and can oscillate. It isn't differentiable at zero, and it predicts the median, which is wrong if the business needs the mean (expected revenue, for example). Huber gives most of the robustness with smoother optimisation.

5.What's the difference between an outlier and a high-leverage point?

An outlier has an unusual y given its x. A high-leverage point has an unusual x. A point is influential when removing it changes the fit a lot, which usually takes both. Cook's distance measures that influence.
